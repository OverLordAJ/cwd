A Data Essay on Social Media

The Great Unraveling of the Platforms We Built Our Lives On

In a single year, TikTok rewrote the rules of online attention, Twitter self-destructed in the spotlight, and Meta lost hundreds of billions of dollars chasing a vision nobody asked for. The data tells a story about power — and how fast it shifts.

By Amiel Russell Published April 2025 Course 301S
Scroll

01 — The Setup

Three platforms. Billions of users. One tipping point.

For most of the 2010s, the social media landscape felt permanent. Facebook dominated nearly every corner of the internet, Twitter served as the world's public square, and Instagram quietly turned into a cultural mirror — reflecting how we dressed, ate, traveled, and presented ourselves. These weren't just apps. They were infrastructure.

Then came 2022. In twelve months, a Chinese-owned video app pulled more daily attention than anything Silicon Valley had produced. The platform that once hosted presidents and breaking news descended into operational chaos after a $44 billion acquisition. And the company behind Facebook, already reeling from user decline, watched its market value evaporate by nearly 70 percent. The ground that had felt so solid turned out to be anything but.

Researchers at Northeastern University saw it coming. "Social media success is a game of exponential growth where platforms rise and fall rapidly once they gain or lose traction," said Yakov Bart, an associate professor of marketing. "It's entirely plausible that a platform we've barely noticed today will be the biggest talk of the town tomorrow."

What the data from that period reveals is something more fundamental than corporate drama: a restructuring of how human attention is allocated online, and who controls it.

"Social media is such an integral part of modern life that any change on a platform can be seismic."

— Northeastern Global News, December 2022

02 — The Numbers

TikTok's rise wasn't a fluke. It was a takeover.

When researchers look at the user growth curves of major social platforms between 2019 and 2023, one line bends differently than all the others. TikTok's ascent from 500 million to over 1.5 billion monthly active users in four years represents the fastest adoption of any entertainment platform in history — outpacing even the peak years of Facebook and YouTube.

What's particularly striking isn't TikTok's raw numbers, but what happened to everyone else's. Twitter plateaued and then contracted. Facebook's growth flattened near the three-billion mark. Only Instagram, benefiting from Meta's aggressive push of short-form Reels, kept meaningful momentum. The chart tells a story of a finite pool of human attention — and who's winning the fight to hold it.

"One could imagine that the company could start to get squeezed by regulators. TikTok may become more of a political football."

— John Wihbey, Northeastern University

TikTok's growth has never been purely about entertainment. Its algorithm — which surfaces content based on behavioral signals rather than social connections — represented a fundamental departure from how platforms like Facebook and Twitter were built. You don't need to follow anyone. You don't need to know anyone. The feed finds you.

03 — Meta's Fall

A $700 billion evaporation

In February 2022, Meta reported that Facebook had lost daily active users for the first time in its history. One million people — gone in a single quarter. The stock dropped 26% in a single day, erasing roughly $230 billion in market value overnight. It was the largest single-day market cap loss for any company in U.S. stock market history.

By November of that year, the stock had fallen nearly 70% from its peak. The culprit, according to analysts, was a combination of Apple's iOS privacy changes cutting into ad revenue, growing competition from TikTok, and deep investor skepticism about Mark Zuckerberg's bet on the metaverse — a virtual reality world that cost the company over $10 billion in 2021 alone, with little to show for it publicly.

"Facebook can't succeed anymore unless they invent something that's used by multiple billions of people," said Rahul Bhargava, assistant professor at Northeastern. "There's only so many things that could be used by multiple billions of people." The company's recovery in 2023, driven by a year of aggressive cost-cutting Zuckerberg called the "Year of Efficiency," showed how quickly markets forgive — but not how quickly trust returns.

"Certainly, they're trying to figure out if they can copy the 'secret sauce' of TikTok."

— Yakov Bart, Northeastern University

04 — The Attention Economy

Where people actually spend their time tells a different story

User counts only capture part of the picture. A platform can have a billion registered accounts and still be losing the attention war. The more revealing metric is daily engagement — how many minutes per day the average user spends on each platform. And by that measure, TikTok isn't just growing. It's dominant in a way that should alarm every other app fighting for screen time.

In 2022, TikTok users spent an average of 95 minutes per day on the platform — nearly twice the time spent on YouTube, and more than three times what Facebook or Instagram commanded. Those aren't marginal differences. They represent structural advantages in the attention economy that are extraordinarily hard to compete against.

"TikTok doesn't change the underlying concern that we're engineering platforms to harness human attention in ways that are somewhat deceptive, probably harmful — not great for young people."

— John Wihbey, Northeastern University

The engagement gap matters because advertising revenue follows attention. Every extra minute a user spends scrolling TikTok is a minute they aren't spending on Facebook or Twitter. As those engagement curves diverged through 2022 and 2023, the business model underpinning older platforms became harder to sustain — and the pressure to algorithmically optimize content, regardless of its social effects, became more intense.

05 — What It Means

The permanence was always an illusion

What the data from 2022 ultimately reveals is that social media platforms are not utilities — they're not water systems or electricity grids that persist regardless of what's happening inside the company. They are, at their core, attention machines. And attention is fickle, contextual, and remarkably easy to redirect once something better comes along.

The predictions made by Northeastern researchers at the end of 2022 aged in interesting ways. TikTok didn't get banned nationally in the United States — not in 2023, not even in 2024, despite ongoing Congressional pressure. Twitter, renamed X under Musk's ownership, retained a core user base but shed the cultural centrality it once had. Meta's stock recovered dramatically in 2023, propelled by AI-driven advertising optimization and a genuine reversal of its spending trajectory, but questions about its long-term relevance among younger users persisted.

Perhaps most significantly, the Mastodon moment — the brief, hopeful surge of decentralized social media — showed both that alternatives were possible and that network effects made them brutally hard to sustain. People went back. They always go back, until they don't.

The deeper lesson isn't about any single platform. It's about what we choose to pay attention to, and what that attention funds. Every minute spent scrolling is a vote — for an algorithm, for an ad model, for a set of content moderation decisions made by a small group of people with enormous power over what information billions of people see. The platforms may have felt permanent. The choices we make on them never were.
